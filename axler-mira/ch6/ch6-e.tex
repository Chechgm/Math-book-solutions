\begin{exercise}{5}
Suppose $X = \set{0} \cup \bigcup_{k = 1}^\infty \set{1/k}$ and $d(x, y) = \absoluteValue{x - y}$ for $x, y \in X$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Show that $(X, d)$ is a complete metric space.
    \item Each set of the form $\set{x}$ for $x \in X$ is a closed subset of $\R$ that has an empty interior as a subset of $\R$.
    Clearly $X$ is a countable union of such sets.
    Explain why this does not violate the statement of Baire's Theorem that a complete metric space is not the countable union of closed subsets with empty interior.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{proof}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Any Cauchy sequence, say $(x_n)$ in $X$ must either be eventually constant or get closer to 0.
    If this were not the case, then there would exist an $\epsilon > 0$ so that for all $n, m \in \N$, we would have $\absoluteValue{x_n - x_m} \geq \epsilon$.
    Since 0 belongs to $X$, then $X$ is complete.
    \item This doesn't contradict Baire's Theorem, given that for any $x \neq 0$, $\overline{\set{x}}$ does not have an empty interior.
    In particular, if $x = 1/k$, then $B_r(x) \subseteq \overline{\set{1/k}}$, where $r = 1/(k+1)^2$.
    The lesson here is that the ambient space matters when thinking about whether a closed subset has or not an empty interior, as we saw previously in Carothers' Real Analysis.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof} 

\begin{exercise}{6}
Give an example of a metric space that is the countable union of closed subsets with empty interior.

[This exercise shows that the completeness hypothesis in Baire's Theorem cannot be dropped.]
\end{exercise}
\begin{proof}
$\Q = \bigcup_{r \in \Q} \overline{\set{r}} = \bigcup_{r \in \Q} \set{r}$, understood as a metric subspace of $\R$.
\end{proof}
